Chapter 1: Research Vision and Hypothesis
1.1 Unified Research Direction
The proposed research direction unifies two critical domains of Earth system science:
drought dynamics modeling and crop phenology monitoring. Rather than treating these as
separate problems, the AgroEarthFM project posits that their intersection creates a
fundamentally richer and more impactful scientific contribution. By building a self-
supervised multimodal physics-informed causal geo foundation model, we aim to learn the
complete causal chain from climate forcing through ecological response to agricultural
consequence. This unified framing addresses a critical gap in current geospatial AI, where
existing foundation models capture surface appearance but fail to model causal agro-
ecosystem processes linking climate stress, vegetation dynamics, and agricultural
resilience.
The merger is scientifically powerful because it bridges slow environmental stress
processes with biological growth cycles, connects climate causality to agricultural
outcomes, and links ecosystem resilience directly to food security. This creates what we
term a complete causal Earth-food system model, which is qualitatively different from and
substantially more impactful than either a drought-only or crop-only model. The drought
domain provides the physical drivers and stress mechanisms, while the crop phenology
domain provides the biological response functions and outcome metrics. Together, they
enable a model that does not merely classify or detect, but rather understands process
dynamics in a physically grounded and causally directed manner.
Current geospatial foundation models such as Prithvi-EO-2.0 and DOFA have
demonstrated the power of pretraining on large-scale Earth observation data, but they
remain fundamentally appearance-based. They learn what a scene looks like rather than
what processes are occurring and why. AgroEarthFM addresses this limitation by
explicitly incorporating physical constraints and causal structure into the learning process,
enabling the model to learn not just correlations but the directional dependencies that
govern agro-ecosystem behavior under climate stress.
1.2 Core Scientific Hypothesis
Self-supervised multimodal representations that jointly model drought dynamics and
crop phenology under physical constraints can learn causal agro-ecosystem processes that
improve robustness, transferability, and agricultural resilience prediction across climate
regions.

This hypothesis makes three strong claims that the research must validate. First, that
joint modeling of drought and phenology is superior to modeling either in isolation, which
addresses the representation quality question. Second, that physics-informed constraints
improve the learned representations beyond what data-driven learning alone can achieve,

addresses the robustness and out-of-distribution performance question. Each of these
claims is testable through controlled experiments comparing against single-domain
baselines, unconstrained multimodal baselines, and correlation-only baselines respectively.
1.3 Why This Merge Is Powerful
The integration of drought dynamics with crop phenology creates five powerful
synergistic bridges across the climate-food system. The drought component contributes
slow environmental stress processes, climate causality, ecosystem resilience assessment,
and hydrological process modeling. The crop phenology component contributes biological
growth cycles, agricultural outcome prediction, food security assessment, and plant
physiological understanding. When these are modeled jointly, the resulting framework
captures the full spectrum from physical driver to biological consequence to societal
impact, which is precisely the kind of integrated understanding that the Earth system
science community has identified as a critical need.
This unified approach also has practical advantages for model training. Drought
indicators such as soil moisture decline and evapotranspiration anomalies provide strong
temporal signals that can anchor self-supervised learning objectives. Crop phenology
transitions such as emergence, flowering, and senescence provide discrete, verifiable
events that can serve as natural supervision signals. The combination means the model has
access to both continuous process dynamics and discrete state transitions, which together
provide a richer set of pretraining objectives than either domain alone.
1.4 The Complete Causal Chain
The central organizing principle of AgroEarthFM is a complete causal chain that runs
from climate forcing through multiple intermediate states to food security risk. This chain
is not merely a conceptual diagram but represents the actual information flow that the
model must learn to represent. The chain proceeds as follows: climate forcing produces
temperature anomalies, which drive evapotranspiration imbalance, leading to soil moisture
decline, which causes vegetation stress, resulting in phenology disruption, leading to crop
stress accumulation, culminating in yield reduction and ultimately food security risk. Each

Chapter 2: Model Architecture Design
2.1 Overall Architecture Overview
The AgroEarthFM architecture follows a multi-stage pipeline design: inputs are
processed by modality-specific encoders, fused into a shared agro-ecosystem state
representation, refined through physics-informed and causal reasoning layers, and decoded
by task-specific output heads. This design is motivated by the observation that different
input modalities have fundamentally different statistical properties and temporal
characteristics. Satellite imagery is spatially structured but temporally sparse; weather data
is temporally dense but spatially coarse; soil and crop calendar information is categorical
or semi-structured. A single monolithic encoder would struggle to handle this diversity,
whereas specialized encoders can exploit the structure of each modality before fusion.

The architecture is designed around five core principles. First, modality-specific
encoding ensures that each data stream is processed by an encoder suited to its structure.
Second, cross-modal fusion through attention mechanisms allows the model to learn how
different modalities interact and influence each other. Third, temporal memory captures
the slow dynamics of drought progression and the seasonal cycles of crop development.
Fourth, physics-informed constraints ensure that the learned representations respect
conservation laws and process constraints. Fifth, causal reasoning layers impose
directional structure on the relationships between variables, moving beyond correlation
toward genuine process understanding.
2.2 Modality-Specific Encoders
2.2.1 Satellite Imagery Encoder (ViT)
The satellite imagery encoder is based on the Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture,
adapted for multispectral geospatial data. Unlike natural images, satellite imagery contains
10 or more spectral bands spanning visible, near-infrared, and shortwave-infrared
wavelengths, each carrying distinct physical information about surface properties. The
encoder processes these bands as multi-channel inputs to the ViT, with band-specific patch
embeddings that preserve the physical meaning of each spectral channel. This design
follows recent geospatial ViT implementations such as Prithvi-EO-2.0, which
demonstrated that treating spectral bands as structured inputs rather than generic channels
improves downstream performance on Earth observation tasks.
The ViT encoder is also designed to handle the temporal dimension of satellite data.
Each observation at a given time step is processed independently through the spatial
encoder, producing a set of spatial token embeddings. These tokens are then passed to the
temporal memory module, which tracks how spatial patterns evolve over time. This

separation of spatial and temporal processing is computationally efficient and allows the
model to handle irregular observation frequencies, which are common in satellite data due
to cloud cover and revisit schedules.
2.2.2 Weather Data Encoder (Temporal Transformer)
Weather variables such as temperature, rainfall, humidity, solar radiation, and wind
are inherently temporal and are typically available at much higher temporal resolution than
satellite imagery. The weather encoder uses a temporal Transformer architecture that
processes sequences of weather observations over the same time window as the satellite
data. Each time step is represented as a feature vector of weather variables, and the
Transformer applies self-attention across time to capture temporal dependencies such as
heat waves, dry spells, and seasonal transitions. The encoder outputs a sequence of
weather state embeddings that capture both short-term weather patterns and long-term
climate trends.
2.2.3 Soil Data Encoder (MLP/GNN)
Soil variables including soil moisture, soil type, and groundwater level are spatially
structured but vary on different spatial scales than satellite imagery. The soil encoder uses
a combination of MLP layers for point-wise soil properties and Graph Neural Network
(GNN) layers for spatially distributed properties. The GNN component is particularly
important for modeling soil moisture dynamics, which are influenced by topography,
drainage networks, and spatially varying soil properties. The encoder outputs a soil state
embedding that captures both the current soil conditions and their spatial context.
2.2.4 Crop Calendar Encoder (Embedding)
Crop calendar information provides strong prior knowledge about expected phenology
stages and management activities. The crop calendar encoder maps crop type, planting
date, expected growth stages, and irrigation status into a learned embedding space. This
embedding serves as a conditional input to the rest of the model, biasing the model toward
phenologically appropriate interpretations of the satellite and weather data. For example, if
the crop calendar indicates that the growing season has just begun, the model should
interpret low NDVI values as expected rather than as stress signals.
2.2.5 Spatial Topology Encoder (Graph Encoder)
Agricultural landscapes are not isolated pixels; they exist within spatial networks of
fields, water bodies, and topographic features. The spatial topology encoder uses a graph
structure where nodes represent spatial locations and edges represent spatial adjacency or
hydrological connectivity. This encoder captures the spatial context that influences how
drought stress propagates across a landscape, how irrigation in one area affects

spatial context embeddings that are combined with the per-location encodings from other
modalities.
2.3 Cross-Modal Fusion Layer
The cross-modal fusion layer is the central integrative component of AgroEarthFM. It
takes the embeddings from all modality-specific encoders and produces a unified agro-
ecosystem state representation. The fusion mechanism is based on cross-attention, where
each modality can attend to the representations of other modalities. This allows the model
to learn, for example, that a particular pattern of temperature and rainfall (weather
embedding) should modify the interpretation of a satellite observation (imagery
embedding), or that soil moisture conditions (soil embedding) mediate the relationship
between weather and vegetation stress.
The fusion layer uses a stack of cross-attention blocks, each consisting of cross-
attention between modality pairs followed by a feed-forward network. The ordering and
pairing of cross-attention operations are designed to reflect the causal structure of the
system: weather influences soil, soil and weather jointly influence vegetation, and
vegetation state combined with crop calendar determines phenology interpretation. This
causally-informed fusion ordering is a key design choice that biases the model toward
learning physically meaningful interactions rather than arbitrary cross-modal correlations.
2.4 Temporal Memory Module
Agro-ecosystem processes unfold over timescales ranging from days (weather events)
to months (drought progression) to seasons (crop development cycles). The temporal
memory module is designed to capture these multiple timescales using a hierarchical
temporal structure. Short-term dynamics are captured by a recurrent mechanism with a
relatively short memory window, while long-term trends and seasonal patterns are
captured by a separate pathway with longer context. The two pathways are combined to
produce a temporal state embedding that captures both the immediate context and the
longer-term trajectory of agro-ecosystem development.
This design is motivated by the observation that drought impacts on crops are often
cumulative: a single dry week may have minimal impact, but prolonged moisture deficit
over months can be devastating even if individual weeks are not extreme. Similarly, crop
phenology is driven by accumulated growing degree days rather than instantaneous
temperature. The hierarchical temporal memory allows the model to naturally capture
these cumulative effects without requiring manual feature engineering of lagged variables
or accumulated indices.

2.5 Physics-Informed Layer
The physics-informed layer constrains the model representations to respect known
physical laws and conservation principles. The primary physics backbone is the water
balance equation, which states that soil water availability equals precipitation minus
evapotranspiration minus runoff, with adjustments for infiltration and deep percolation.
This constraint is implemented as a soft penalty in the loss function: if the model predicts a
soil moisture state that is inconsistent with the water balance given the observed
precipitation and estimated evapotranspiration, the loss function applies a penalty
proportional to the magnitude of the inconsistency.
A secondary physics constraint comes from plant growth dynamics, which depend on
temperature, water availability, and solar radiation. The model is penalized for predicting
healthy vegetation growth under severe water deficit unless there is evidence of irrigation.
This constraint prevents the model from learning physically impossible states and
encourages it to attribute vegetation changes to their actual physical causes rather than
spurious correlations. The physics-informed layer also provides a natural mechanism for
the model to learn about unobserved variables: if the observed vegetation state is
inconsistent with observed weather and soil conditions, the model can infer the presence of
unobserved factors such as irrigation, deep soil moisture, or pest damage.
2.6 Causal Reasoning Layer
The causal reasoning layer sits on top of the fused and physics-constrained
representations and imposes directional structure on the relationships between variables. It
implements a learned structural causal model over a set of key variables: rainfall,
temperature, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, vegetation health, crop stage, and yield.
The causal graph structure can be partially specified based on domain knowledge (for
example, rainfall causes soil moisture increase, not the reverse) and partially learned from
data (for example, the relative strength of temperature versus rainfall effects on crop stress
may vary by region and season).
The causal layer serves three important functions. First, it makes the model
interpretable by exposing which causal pathways are most active in a given prediction.
Second, it enables counterfactual reasoning: the model can answer questions like "what
would happen to crop stress if rainfall decreased by 20%?" by intervening on the rainfall
node and propagating the effects through the causal graph. Third, it improves
transferability: because causal relationships are more stable across environments than
correlations, a model that captures causal structure should generalize better to new regions
and climate conditions.

2.7 Output Heads
The model produces four distinct outputs, each decoded by a specialized head from
the shared agro-ecosystem state representation. The drought stress score head produces a
continuous scalar indicating the current level of drought stress on a standardized scale. The
phenology forecast head predicts the current growth stage and the timing of upcoming
phenology transitions. The yield risk head estimates the probability of yield falling below
critical thresholds. The resilience score head produces an integrated assessment of the
agro-ecosystem capacity to withstand and recover from climate stress. These four outputs
together provide a comprehensive picture of agro-ecosystem status that is far more
informative than any single classification or regression output.
Chapter 3: Data Pipeline Design
3.1 Multimodal Data Sources
The AgroEarthFM data pipeline integrates six categories of data, each providing
complementary information about agro-ecosystem state and dynamics. The design
principle is that each data source should contribute unique information that is not fully
captured by other sources, and that the combination should enable the model to learn
causal relationships that would be invisible from any single modality.

3.1.1 Satellite Imagery
The primary satellite imagery source is Sentinel-2, which provides 13-band
multispectral data at 10-20m spatial resolution with a 5-day revisit time. Sentinel-2
captures vegetation health, crop development stage, canopy structure changes, and land

3.1.2 Vegetation Indices
Vegetation indices are derived from satellite spectral bands and provide compressed,
physically meaningful summaries of vegetation properties. The five core indices for
AgroEarthFM are NDVI for overall greenness and vegetation density, EVI for vegetation
vigor that is less saturated in dense canopies, NDWI for water stress and canopy water
content, LAI for total biomass and leaf area, and FAPAR for photosynthetic activity and
productivity. These indices serve dual purposes: they are input features for the model and
they provide ground-truth signals for self-supervised learning objectives. The temporal
trajectories of these indices are particularly informative, as they reveal the dynamics of
vegetation stress and recovery that static snapshots cannot capture.

3.2 Time-Aligned Agro-Climate Datacube Construction
The core data structure for AgroEarthFM is the time-aligned agro-climate datacube: a
spatiotemporal array that stacks all input modalities for a given region and time window
into a unified, aligned representation. Building this datacube requires resolving several
alignment challenges. Temporal alignment is needed because satellite imagery, weather
data, and soil moisture observations all have different temporal resolutions and revisit
schedules. Spatial alignment requires resampling all data to a common grid, which
involves decisions about the target spatial resolution and the resampling method. Spectral
alignment is needed when combining data from different satellite missions with different
band definitions.
The datacube construction pipeline proceeds in four stages. First, all data sources are
reprojected to a common coordinate reference system and resampled to a common spatial
grid, with the target resolution determined by the highest-resolution input (typically 10m
from Sentinel-2). Second, temporal alignment is performed by defining regular time steps
(e.g., 5-day or 10-day composites) and aggregating or interpolating all observations to
these time steps. Third, quality filtering removes cloudy pixels, missing values, and low-
quality observations, with gap-filling performed using temporal interpolation or spatial
neighborhood methods. Fourth, derived variables such as vegetation indices and drought
indices are computed from the aligned base variables.
3.3 Data Preprocessing and Quality Control
Quality control is critical for geospatial data, which is subject to cloud contamination,
atmospheric effects, sensor artifacts, and missing observations. The preprocessing pipeline
applies cloud masking using Sentinel-2 scene classification layers, atmospheric correction
validation, temporal consistency checks that flag unrealistic jumps in vegetation index
values, and spatial coherence checks that identify isolated anomalous pixels. For climate
data, quality control includes checking for station artifacts in reanalysis products,
validating extreme value plausibility, and ensuring temporal completeness. The goal is to
provide the model with clean, consistent data that reflects actual surface and atmospheric
conditions rather than observation artifacts.

3.4 Temporal Alignment Strategy
The temporal alignment strategy must balance several competing requirements. Fine
temporal resolution captures rapid events such as flash droughts and phenology transitions,
but increases computational cost and may introduce noise from sparse observations.
Coarse temporal resolution provides smoother trajectories but may miss critical events.
The proposed strategy uses a two-level temporal representation: a fine-grained level at 5-
day intervals for capturing rapid dynamics, and a coarse-grained level at monthly intervals
for capturing seasonal and long-term trends. The model processes both levels
simultaneously through the hierarchical temporal memory module, with the fine-grained
pathway providing detail and the coarse-grained pathway providing context.
Chapter 4: Self-Supervised Training Strategy
4.1 Process-Centered SSL Design Philosophy
Standard self-supervised learning in vision uses pretext tasks such as masked
autoencoding (MAE) or contrastive learning, which operate on pixel-level or patch-level
reconstruction and discrimination. While effective for learning appearance representations,
these objectives are insufficient for learning process dynamics. AgroEarthFM introduces
process-centered SSL, where the pretext tasks are designed to force the model to learn the
temporal, causal, and physical processes that govern agro-ecosystem behavior. Each SSL
objective targets a different aspect of process understanding, and together they provide
comprehensive coverage of the causal chain from climate forcing to food security risk.

4.2 Objective 1: Phenology Transition Prediction

The first SSL objective requires the model to predict phenology stage transitions from
temporal observations. Given a sequence of vegetation index observations up to time t, the
model must predict whether a phenology transition (such as emergence, flowering, or
senescence) will occur within a future time window, and if so, which transition. This
objective forces the model to learn the temporal patterns that precede phenology
transitions, including the environmental conditions (temperature accumulation, water
availability) that trigger stage changes. The training signal comes from the natural
temporal structure of the data: transitions can be detected from the temporal derivatives of
vegetation indices even without explicit ground-truth labels, making this a genuinely self-
supervised objective.
4.3 Objective 2: Drought Stress Evolution
The second SSL objective requires the model to predict the trajectory of vegetation
degradation under drought stress. Given observations of a region experiencing developing
drought conditions up to time t, the model must predict the vegetation stress trajectory over
the next several time steps. This objective forces the model to learn the dynamics of
drought progression, including the lagged effects of moisture deficit on vegetation, the
nonlinear threshold effects where small additional stress causes disproportionate
degradation, and the differential vulnerability of different crop types and growth stages.
The training signal comes from the observed temporal evolution of vegetation indices and
drought indices in historically drought-affected regions.
4.4 Objective 3: Cross-Modal Causal Prediction
The third SSL objective tests whether the model has learned cross-modal causal
relationships by requiring it to predict the state of one modality from others. Specifically,
given rainfall and temperature observations, the model must predict the resulting soil
moisture trajectory; given weather and soil conditions, it must predict the vegetation
response. This objective directly tests the model understanding of causal mechanisms
rather than mere correlation. A model that has learned that rainfall increases soil moisture,
which in turn supports vegetation growth, should be able to predict soil moisture decline
from rainfall deficit and vegetation stress from soil moisture decline, even in regions and
conditions not seen during training.
4.5 Objective 4: Future Ecosystem State Prediction
The fourth SSL objective is the most comprehensive: given all available observations
up to time t, predict the full ecosystem state at a future time horizon. This includes
vegetation health, crop stress level, phenology stage, and yield risk indicators. This

objective integrates all the process understanding captured by the previous objectives and
tests whether the model can perform genuine temporal forecasting rather than mere pattern
matching. The prediction horizon is varied during training to encourage the model to
capture dynamics at multiple timescales, from weeks to months to seasons.
4.6 Objective 5: Counterfactual Learning
The fifth and most novel SSL objective is counterfactual learning. Given an observed
sequence of conditions and outcomes, the model is asked to predict what would have
happened under modified conditions: what if rainfall decreased by 20%? What if the
temperature had been 2 degrees higher? What if irrigation had been applied? This
objective forces the model to learn not just what happens under observed conditions, but
why it happens and what would change under different scenarios. Counterfactual learning
is implemented by creating synthetic interventions on the input data (modifying rainfall or
temperature in the input) and requiring the model to predict the resulting changes in
vegetation, soil, and crop outcomes. The training signal comes from the physical
consistency constraints: the counterfactual predictions must satisfy the same water balance,
energy balance, and growth dynamics as factual predictions.
Chapter 5: Physics-Informed Constraints
5.1 Water Balance Physics
The water balance is the foundational physical constraint for AgroEarthFM. It
expresses the conservation of water mass in the soil-plant-atmosphere system and can be
formulated as: soil water availability equals precipitation minus evapotranspiration minus
runoff, plus any irrigation input, minus deep percolation. This equation must hold at all
times and locations, and violations indicate either measurement error, model error, or
unobserved water fluxes. By incorporating this constraint into the training loss, we ensure
that the model representations are physically consistent and that the learned relationships
between precipitation, evapotranspiration, and soil moisture respect mass conservation.
The water balance constraint is implemented as a differentiable loss term that
penalizes the discrepancy between predicted soil moisture changes and the water balance
implied by observed or predicted precipitation, evapotranspiration, and runoff. The
evapotranspiration component itself can be estimated using established methods such as
the Penman-Monteith equation, which provides a physically grounded relationship
between atmospheric conditions and water loss. By using Penman-Monteith
evapotranspiration estimates as a soft constraint rather than a hard requirement, the model

5.2 Plant Growth Physics
Plant growth dynamics provide the second physical constraint. Crop growth depends
on three primary resources: temperature (expressed through growing degree days), water
availability, and solar radiation. When any of these is limiting, growth is constrained
accordingly. The model is penalized for predicting healthy vegetation growth under
conditions where one or more resources are severely limiting, unless there is evidence of
compensating factors such as irrigation or deep root access to groundwater. This constraint
prevents the model from learning physically impossible scenarios and encourages it to
attribute vegetation changes to their actual environmental drivers.
The growth constraint is implemented through a limiting-factor model inspired by the
Mitscherlich-Baule production function, where growth is proportional to the most limiting
resource. This creates a differentiable constraint that can be incorporated into the training
loss. The model is also encouraged to learn about phenology-specific resource
requirements: for example, water stress during flowering has disproportionately large
effects on yield compared to water stress during vegetative growth. This phenology-
dependent sensitivity is a well-established feature of crop physiology that the model
should capture naturally if it learns the correct causal structure.
5.3 Physics-Regularized Loss Design
The total training loss combines the self-supervised objectives from Chapter 4 with the
physics-informed constraints from this chapter. The loss has the form: L_total = L_ssl +
lambda_physics * L_physics + lambda_causal * L_causal, where L_ssl is the sum of the
five SSL objectives, L_physics combines the water balance and growth constraints, and
L_causal enforces the causal graph structure. The weighting coefficients lambda_physics
and lambda_causal are hyperparameters that control the strength of the physics and causal
constraints relative to the data-driven SSL objectives. These coefficients should be tuned
carefully: too strong and the model may be over-constrained, unable to learn from data; too
weak and the physics and causality become ineffective.

6.1 Causal Graph Structure

crop stage influence each other bidirectionally; and vegetation health and crop stage jointly
determine yield. This graph structure is partially specified based on established domain

6.2 Causal Discovery from Observations
While the causal graph structure is partially specified from domain knowledge, the
strength and functional form of causal relationships must be learned from observational
data. The model uses a neural causal inference approach where the causal graph is
parameterized as a set of neural network modules, each representing the functional
relationship from parent nodes to child nodes. The graph structure can be refined using
differentiable structure learning, where the presence or absence of edges is parameterized
by learnable weights that are optimized alongside the model parameters. This approach
allows the model to discover which causal pathways are most important in different
regions and seasons, providing insights that go beyond the initial domain-knowledge-
specified structure.

6.3 Counterfactual Reasoning Module
The counterfactual reasoning module enables the model to answer what-if questions
about agro-ecosystem responses to hypothetical interventions. Given a factual observation
sequence and a proposed intervention (such as a 20% reduction in rainfall), the module
performs a three-step inference. First, it infers the exogenous noise terms that explain the
factual observations given the learned causal model. Second, it applies the intervention by
replacing the specified variable with its counterfactual value while keeping the exogenous
noise fixed. Third, it propagates the intervention through the causal graph using the learned
structural equations to compute the counterfactual outcomes. This procedure follows the
standard potential-outcomes framework for causal inference but implements it within a
neural network, enabling end-to-end differentiable counterfactual prediction.
6.4 Intervention and What-If Analysis
The ability to perform intervention analysis is one of the most valuable practical
applications of AgroEarthFM. Decision-makers need to understand not just what is
happening now, but what would happen under different scenarios. The model supports
several types of intervention analysis. Climate scenario analysis asks: what would happen
to crop yields under a 2-degree warming scenario? Water management analysis asks: how
much irrigation would be needed to prevent yield loss under a moderate drought?
Adaptation strategy analysis asks: which crop types or management practices would be
most resilient under projected climate conditions? These analyses are enabled by the
causal structure of the model, which allows interventions to be applied at any point in the
causal chain and their effects to be propagated forward through the graph.
Chapter 7: Downstream Tasks and Evaluation
7.1 Primary Downstream Tasks
AgroEarthFM is designed to support six primary downstream tasks, each
corresponding to a critical agricultural and environmental application. These tasks
demonstrate the breadth of the model capabilities and provide concrete evaluation metrics
for each aspect of agro-ecosystem understanding.
1. Drought Forecasting: Predict drought onset, intensity, and duration at lead times
of 1-6 months, using the model temporal and causal representations. This task
evaluates whether the model captures the slow dynamics and precursor signals of
drought development.

--- PAGE 21 ---
AgroEarthFM Implementation Plan

2. Crop Stress Detection: Identify regions and time periods where crops are
experiencing stress from water deficit, heat, or combined factors. This task
evaluates whether the model can distinguish between normal phenology-driven
vegetation changes and stress-driven degradation.
3. Yield Prediction: Estimate crop yield at field to regional scales before harvest,
using the model accumulated stress and phenology representations. This task
evaluates whether the model captures the causal chain from stress to yield impact.
4. Food Insecurity Risk Assessment: Map the spatial distribution of food insecurity
risk based on projected yield shortfalls, drought persistence, and resilience
indicators. This task integrates multiple model outputs into a societal impact
assessment.
5. Phenology Forecasting: Predict the timing of crop growth stage transitions,
including emergence, flowering, and senescence, under current and projected
conditions. This task directly evaluates the phenology SSL objective in a practical
setting.
6. Climate Adaptation Mapping: Identify regions where agricultural practices need
to adapt to changing climate conditions, based on the model causal analysis of
stress drivers and resilience factors. This task leverages the counterfactual
reasoning capability.
7.2 Evaluation Framework
The evaluation framework follows a three-tier structure. Tier 1 assesses representation
quality through linear probing and fine-tuning on standard geospatial benchmarks such as
GEO-Bench and PhilEO Bench. Tier 2 assesses task performance on the six primary
downstream tasks, comparing against domain-specific baselines and existing geospatial
foundation models. Tier 3 assesses physical consistency and causal validity through
physics compliance tests and causal discovery accuracy metrics. This three-tier structure
ensures that the model is evaluated not just on prediction accuracy but also on the quality
of its learned representations and the physical and causal validity of its internal reasoning.
7.3 Benchmarks and Baselines
The model will be compared against several categories of baselines. Task-specific
models represent the current state of the art for each individual downstream task. General
geospatial foundation models such as Prithvi-EO-2.0, DOFA, and SatMAE provide
comparisons for the foundation model approach without domain-specific design. Ablation
models test the contribution of each component by removing or replacing the physics layer,
causal layer, or process-centered SSL objectives. This comprehensive comparison ensures

7.4 Expected Results
Based on the theoretical arguments and the empirical performance of related
approaches in the literature, we expect AgroEarthFM to show three key advantages over
baselines. First, improved transferability: the model should generalize better to new
regions and climate conditions because causal relationships are more stable than
correlations. Second, improved physical consistency: the model predictions should violate
physical constraints less frequently than unconstrained baselines, leading to more
trustworthy outputs for decision-making. Third, improved performance on tail events: the
model should perform better on extreme drought and stress events that are
underrepresented in training data but are precisely the events where accurate prediction
matters most, because the causal structure provides inductive bias that guides correct
prediction even when data is scarce.

Chapter 9: Prerequisites and Learning Path
9.1 Technical Prerequisites
Starting from scratch requires building skills in six major areas, each of which is
essential for different aspects of the AgroEarthFM project. Python and deep learning
fundamentals provide the programming and modeling infrastructure. Geospatial data
handling provides the ability to work with the specific data formats and structures of Earth
observation data. Remote sensing fundamentals provide the domain knowledge needed to
interpret satellite data correctly. Self-supervised learning provides the training
methodology for the foundation model. Physics-informed learning provides the framework
for incorporating physical constraints. Causal inference provides the mathematical
foundations for the causal reasoning layer.
9.2 Phase-by-Phase Learning Path
9.2.1 Phase 1: Python and Deep Learning (Weeks 1-4)
Begin with Python proficiency including NumPy, Pandas, and basic data manipulation.
Then move to PyTorch fundamentals: tensors, autograd, training loops, GPU usage, and
model debugging. The primary resources are the official PyTorch tutorials and
documentation, supplemented by the fast.ai Practical Deep Learning course for practical
intuition. The goal is not just to be able to write training code, but to understand what is
happening at each step and to be able to debug when things go wrong. Practice by
implementing a simple image classifier and a small regression model from scratch.
9.2.2 Phase 2: Geospatial Data Handling (Weeks 3-6)
Learn xarray for labeled multidimensional arrays and rasterio for raster IO. The key
insight is that Earth observation data is not just images; it comes with coordinate reference
systems, metadata, multiple resolutions, and multispectral bands. TorchGeo provides CRS-
aware datasets, spatial samplers, multispectral transforms, and pretrained geospatial
models specifically designed for this reality. Practice by loading Sentinel-2 imagery,
computing vegetation indices, and building a small temporal datacube. The Python
Geospatial and Earth Data Science textbooks provide comprehensive coverage of these
topics.
9.2.3 Phase 3: Remote Sensing Fundamentals (Weeks 5-8)

Learn the major satellite data sources in depth. Sentinel-2 provides 13-band optical
data for land monitoring at 10-20m resolution. Sentinel-1 provides all-weather, day-and-

resolution imagery through USGS. HLS fuses Landsat and Sentinel-2 into an analysis-

9.2.4 Phase 4: Self-Supervised Learning (Weeks 7-12)
Study the canonical SSL families: SimCLR for contrastive learning, DINO for self-

many modern vision and geospatial foundation models. Implement each method on a
simple dataset first, then adapt to geospatial data. The important lesson is not the exact
architecture details, but how pretext tasks change the representation and what each SSL
objective encourages the model to learn. For geospatial work, consider how spatial
augmentation strategies differ from natural image augmentation and how temporal
structure can be exploited for SSL objectives.
9.2.5 Phase 5: Multimodal Geospatial Learning (Weeks 11-16)
Study recent multimodal EO foundation models and surveys. The current EO literature
explicitly moves toward multimodal foundation models and multimodal self-supervised
learning, because single-modality and task-specific models are too limited. Key references
include the FM4EO agenda paper, the multimodal SSL survey for EO, and model papers
for DOFA and TerraMind. Practice by implementing a simple two-modality fusion model
combining optical and SAR data, then extend to include weather data.
9.2.6 Phase 6: Physics-Informed Learning and Causal Inference (Weeks 15-22)
Learn physics-informed neural networks from the original PINN formulation by Raissi
et al. and the comprehensive review by Karniadakis et al. For causal inference, study
causal diagrams and the potential-outcomes framework. Harvard causal-diagrams course
provides a clean entry point, and STAT 186 introduces randomized experiments,
observational inference, and sequential treatments. Practice by adding a simple physics
constraint to a training loop and implementing a basic causal graph over a small set of
variables.

10.3 Mitigation Strategies Summary
The overarching mitigation strategy follows three principles. First, start simple and
add complexity incrementally: validate each component in isolation before integrating it
into the full model. Second, maintain rigorous baselines at every stage: if adding a
component does not improve performance, investigate why before proceeding. Third,
design for interpretability: the causal graph and physics constraints make the model more
interpretable than a black-box alternative, and this interpretability should be leveraged for
debugging and validation. By following these principles, the research can navigate the
substantial technical and data challenges while maintaining scientific rigor and producing
reliable results.
